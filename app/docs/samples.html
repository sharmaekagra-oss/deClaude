2. Blog post

Prompt: Write a short blog post (about 300 words) on why first-time managers struggle with delegation.

Why First-Time Managers Struggle to Delegate

Most people get promoted into management because they were good at the work. That's exactly what makes delegation so hard.

The skill that got you here is now the trap. As an individual contributor, your value came from doing things well and doing them yourself. When you become a manager, your instinct is still to solve problems directly. Handing a task to someone else can feel like giving away the part of the job you're actually confident in.

"It's faster if I just do it." In the short term, this is often true. Explaining a task, answering questions, and reviewing someone else's work takes longer than finishing it yourself. But this math only holds once. Every time you skip delegating, you guarantee you'll be doing that task again next week, and your team never builds the ability to take it off your plate.

Fear of losing control. New managers often worry that if the work isn't done their way, it won't be done right. Sometimes that's a quality concern. Often it's discomfort with the fact that there are many acceptable ways to do something, and yours isn't the only one.

Not knowing what "good delegation" looks like. Many first-time managers swing between two extremes: dumping a task with no context, or handing it over and then hovering. Neither works. Good delegation means being clear about the outcome, the constraints, and the deadline, then stepping back while staying available.

Guilt about adding to people's workload. Some managers hold on to tasks because they don't want to seem like they're offloading work. But delegation, done well, is how people grow. Holding back stretch assignments can quietly stall your team's development.

The shift is a mental one. Your job is no longer to be the best at the work. It's to build a team that can do the work without you. Delegation is how that happens.

One year of business school, done.

When I started, I thought the biggest challenge would be the coursework. It turned out the bigger lessons came from everything around it.

A few things I'm taking with me into year two:

1. You can't do everything, so choose deliberately. Classes, clubs, competitions, placements prep, friendships. There's always more on the table than there is time. Learning to say no (and being okay with it) was one of the most useful things I picked up.

2. Your peers are your best teachers. Some of my most valuable learning happened outside the classroom, in late-night group discussions and from classmates who came in with completely different backgrounds and ways of thinking.

3. Frameworks are a starting point, not the answer. Case after case showed me that real problems are messy. The frameworks help you structure your thinking, but judgment is what gets you to a good decision.

4. Getting comfortable with being wrong is a skill. Cold calls, presentations, feedback on submissions. There were plenty of moments where I got it wrong in front of others. Each one made the next one easier.

5. Rest is part of the work. I learned this the hard way. Burning out doesn't make you more productive, it just makes everything harder.

Grateful to my professors, my batchmates, and everyone who made this year what it was. Looking forward to what year two brings.

If you've been through business school, what's one thing you wish you'd known at the end of your first year?
